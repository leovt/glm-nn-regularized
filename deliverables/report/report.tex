\begin{abstract}
This project investigates regularized hybrids of generalised linear models
and neural networks for insurance claim frequency pricing.
The proposed model trains a linear branch and a neural correction jointly,
with a penalty on the correction's dense-layer weights.
The results suggest that regularization can recover
baseline-like predictions, but do not establish improved interpretability
or a statistically significant advantage over the benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation and research question}
Generalised linear models (GLMs) are a well studied family of models used in actuarial modelling. Together with a log-link funcunction they create easyly explainable models with a multiplicative structure.

They are a form of machine learning, albeit typically with low dimensionality of the parameter space and much expert input through feature selection and preprocessing.

Feed-forward neural networks (FNNs) represent much larger function spaces, allowing  more flexible models. However more care must be taken not to overfit the training data, which would reduce their predictive power, and they are mostly black boxes, making an explanation of the relationship between features and predicted outcomes difficult.

Recently developped hybrid models try to combine the robustness and explainability of GLM with the flexibility of FNNs. One approach is a combined actuarial neural network (CANN), where a FNN adds a learned correction to a fitted GLM \cite{aitools,nesting}.

This project asks whether joint training with asymmetric regularization
can retain predictive improvements while favouring a GLM-like structure.
Training the GLM jointly with the regularized neural network, it might absorb linear effects which could otherwise be modelled in the neural network in addition to the non linear correction.

\subsection{Contribution and scope}
The empirical contribution is a jointly trained GLM--neural network hybrid, where a regularization parameter controls the balance between the full neural network flexibility and the directly explainable GLM parameters.
The model is trained on French motor third-party liability data, the same dataset used in an exploration of CANN approch by the data science group of the Swiss asscociation of actuaries. A sweep of different regularization parameters is performed.

The analysis concerns claim frequency only.

\section{Background and related work}
\subsection{Actuarial learning and hybrid models}
\emph{AI Tools for Actuaries} supplies the main methodological and
implementation starting point \cite{aitools,resources}.
\emph{Statistical Foundations of Actuarial Learning and its Applications}
is a broader reference for GLMs, neural networks and regularization
\cite{foundations}.
Schelldorfer and W\"uthrich provide the foundational reference for nesting
classical actuarial models in neural networks \cite{nesting}.
They discuss both joint training of the GLM and neural components and a
variant that freezes the fitted GLM coefficients \cite{nesting}.
The CANN benchmark adopted here from \emph{AI Tools for Actuaries} uses
the frozen variant: the fitted GLM provides a fixed reference predictor
and the neural branch learns an additive correction on the log-rate scale
\cite{aitools,resources}.
This project investigates the jointly trained variant with an asymmetric
penalty on the neural correction's dense-layer weights.
The experimental question is how this asymmetric regularization affects
predictive performance and the contribution of the neural branch.

\subsection{Statistical continuity between GLMs and neural networks}
Moving from a GLM to an FNN changes the predictor while retaining the
Poisson assumption, log link, exposure treatment and likelihood-based
evaluation used here.
This provides a direct connection with familiar actuarial modelling.
Greater predictor flexibility does not itself resolve questions about
the suitability of the Poisson assumption or calibration.

\subsection{Feature construction}
The GLM uses driver-age groups, vehicle brand, fuel type, log population
density and ordinal area, yielding 19 encoded predictors plus an intercept.
Driver age 31--40 is the reference group.
Although preprocessing also constructs vehicle-power, vehicle-age and
bonus--malus variables, the fitted GLM formula does not use them.

The neural branch additionally uses vehicle age, bonus--malus, vehicle
power and region, and uses continuous rather than grouped driver age.
Vehicle age, driver age, bonus--malus and power are capped at 20, 90, 150
and 15 respectively and standardised.
Log density is rounded to two decimal places and standardised; area is
ordinally encoded and standardised.
Vehicle brand and region receive two-dimensional embeddings.
Preprocessors are fitted on the full learning sample.
Consequently, differences between neural models and the GLM reflect both
functional flexibility and access to additional predictors.

\section{Model specifications and estimation}
\subsection{Poisson frequency model}
All models use the same statistical model, a Poisson model with log link.
The difference lies in the specification of the linear predictor $\eta(x_i)$, which is a linear function for the GLM and a combination of linear and neural functions for the hybrids.

For claim count $Y_i$, exposure $e_i>0$ and predictors $x_i$, assume
\[
 Y_i\mid X_i=x_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu_i),\qquad
 \mu_i=e_i r_i,\qquad r_i=\exp\{\eta(x_i)\}.
\]
Exposure enters the GLM as a log offset.
The neural models predict annualised rates and fit $Y_i/e_i$ with
exposure weights using the Keras Poisson loss.

\subsection{Implementing GLM in Keras}
A hybrid model introduces two implementation challenges.
Firstly, as the GLM parameters are to be re-trained together with the FNN,
the stochastic gradient descent must be implemented in a way that includes the GLM parameters in the gradient computation.

The GLM can be implemented as a single dense layer with exponential activation. For this experiment, the exponential activation
function was implemented in a separate layer, thus allowing the GLM implementation to be reused in the hybrid model.

The bigger second challenge is to make sure that the GLM parameters are estimated reasonably well by the stochastic gradient descent optimiser, and not drift away from the maximum likelihood estimates.
Before training hybrid models, several experiments were conducted to train a GLM in Keras, using NADAM stochastic gradient descent optimiser.

Table~\ref{tab:glm_keras} shows that suitable hyperparameter settings
can keep the Keras GLM close to the maximum likelihood estimates.
Small batch sizes can nevertheless introduce considerable drift, even
when training starts at the optimum of the convex objective.

The following model calibration experiments were conducted:
\begin{itemize}
\item {GLM: maximum likelihood estimate using iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), implemented in \texttt{statsmodels}.}
\item {Keras Control: Keras implementation of the GLM, parameters initialised with the maximum likelihood estimates, no training performed. (This tests the correct representation of the model function in Keras.)}
\item {Keras Trained: Keras implementation of the GLM, parameters randomly initialised and trained using Nadam optimiser.}
\item {Keras Stability Test: Keras implementation of the GLM, parameters initialised with the maximum likelihood estimates and further trained using Nadam optimiser. (This tests whether stochastic gradient descent can drift away from the maximum likelihood estimates.)}
\end{itemize}

The experiments were conducted with and without a validation split, and with different batch sizes. In case of the randomly initialised models the best epoch was kept for final evaluation, in case of the drift tests the last epoch was kept.

The following table shows the results of the experiments, with the learning and test Poisson deviance, and the observed over expected claims ratio (O/E) on the test data.
Calibration is measured as the ratio of observed over expected claims, with a value of one indicating aggregate balance.
This does not establish calibration within individual risk groups.

\section{Modelling functions}

\subsection{GLM}
The reference GLM is defined as a linear predictor function followed by the inverse of the link function.
In the case of the Poisson GLM, the log link function is used, the canonical link for the Poisson distribution.

The log link function has the advantage that the resulting model is multiplicative, each input factor can be seen as contributing a multiplicative effect on the resulting claims rate.

For comparing the GLM with other models it is important not to neglect the role of input representation and preprocessing, often incorporating expert knowledge and judgement.
Figure~\ref{fig:glm} shows the three important stages in the data flow, the data processing on the left, the linear prediction in the middle, and the exponential output on the right.

\subsection{Feed-forward neural network (FNN)}
The FNN has three hidden layers of widths 20, 15 and 10 with hyperbolic
tangent activations, followed by an exponential rate output.
The frozen CANN multiplies the fitted GLM rate by an exponential neural
correction, equivalently adding that correction on the log-rate scale.
The stand-alone FNN uses the same constructor with the GLM multiplier
replaced by one.

The same hidden-layer architecture is used in the stand-alone FNN,
the frozen CANN and the jointly trained hybrid.
It follows the \emph{AI Tools for Actuaries} implementation.
Figure~\ref{fig:fnn} shows the preprocessing, hidden layers and
exponential output.

Instead of one-hot dummy encoding the FNN uses a learned embedding, which maps categorical inputs to learned lower-dimensional representations. The embedding is trained as part of the model training.

The FNN receives additional features, so the benchmark comparison also
reflects different input information.

\subsection{CANN and regularized mixed GLM-FNN}
Both models combine a linear log-rate predictor with a neural correction.
The functions expressed by the CANN and the regularized hybrid are identical, their representation in code
differs in the order of mixing and exponentiation.
Multiplying rates and adding log-rate predictors are algebraically equivalent.
While the functions are equivalent, the calibration of the
model parameters is different.
The CANN fixes the fitted GLM component, whereas the regularized hybrid
trains both branches jointly and penalises the neural dense-layer weights.
These estimation choices are the main experimental distinction.

As the CANN implementation (figure \ref{fig:cann}) uses fixed GLM predictions, not merely the GLM coefficients, it is convenient
to implement the mixing step as a multiplication of the GLM rate with a correction factor given by an exponential of the neural network log-rate correction.
The GLM does not need to be implemented in the same framework as the FNN, as it is not re-trained and the GLM prediction is fixed.

\section{Implementation}



\subsection{Jointly trained regularized hybrid}
Both branches use transformations of the original predictors $x_i$.
Let $z_i=z(x_i)$ denote the GLM design vector and $u_i=u(x_i)$ the
preprocessed neural inputs.
The implemented predictor is
\[
 \eta(x_i)=z(x_i)^\top\beta+g_\theta(u(x_i)).
\]
The GLM coefficients initialise the linear branch.
The correction's final kernel is initialised to zero, so the initial
prediction equals the GLM prediction; the hidden layers are not all
initialised to zero.
Both branches are trainable.
The implemented objective is
\[
 L(\beta,\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
 e_i\left[r_i-\frac{Y_i}{e_i}\log r_i\right]
 +\lambda\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal D}\lVert W_\ell\rVert_F^2,
\]
where $\mathcal D$ comprises the correction's hidden and output dense
layers.
The penalty excludes embeddings and hidden-layer biases; the correction
output has no bias.
This differs from the outline's proposed penalty on all correction
parameters and should not be described as full-parameter ridge shrinkage.
The linear branch is unpenalised.

\section{Experimental design and evaluation}
\subsection{Training and validation}
Neural-network experiments were run in Google Colab using an NVIDIA T4 GPU.
Neural runs use Nadam and seed 1234.
The principal FNN and CANN runs use 200 epochs and batches of 5,000;
the regularization sweep uses 200 epochs and batches of 50,000.
Ten percent of the learning sample is reserved through
\texttt{validation\_split=0.1} in these runs.
The callback restores the best validation-loss weights; its patience
equals the epoch budget, so it does not normally shorten training.
For penalised models, monitored validation loss includes the penalty.

Moreover, the initial GLM and preprocessing use the full learning sample,
including the neural validation subset.
That subset is therefore not independent of all fitting steps.
Reported learning-sample metrics include the validation observations.
% For a revised experiment, split before all fitting and preserve an
% independent validation sample for every model and preprocessing step.

\subsection{Performance measures}
For observed rates $q_i=Y_i/e_i$, the reported score is
\[
 D_{100}=\frac{200}{\sum_i e_i}\sum_i e_i
 \left[q_i\log\left(\frac{q_i}{r_i}\right)-q_i+r_i\right],
\]
with $0\log(0/r_i)=0$.
This is 100 times the exposure-weighted mean Poisson deviance of rates,
not the unweighted mean deviance of counts.
Aggregate calibration is reported as observed divided by expected claims,
$\sum_iY_i/\sum_i e_i r_i$ (O/E).
A value of one denotes aggregate balance, without implying subgroup
calibration.
Gini indices and lift curves were proposed but are not present in the
saved outputs.

\subsection{FNN correction}
In order to better understand the contribution of the FNN part of the hybrid model,
we look at summary statistics of the FNN contribution on the training data.
Recall that the hybrid predictor is
\[
 \eta(x_i)=z(x_i)^\top\beta+g_\theta(u(x_i)).
\]
The quantity examined is the neural correction $g_\theta(u(x_i))$,
whose root-mean-square over the $n$ learning-sample observations is
\[
 \operatorname{RMS}(g_\theta)
 =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g_\theta(u(x_i))^2}.
\]
Each observation receives equal weight, irrespective of exposure.
The root-mean-square (RMS) summarises the magnitude of the correction
across observations without allowing positive and negative corrections
to cancel.
It is measured on the linear-predictor (log-rate) scale, so it does not
directly represent an average percentage change in predicted rates.

The correction statistics confirm the very abrupt transition from a model where the FNN has a contribution
to where the contribution essentially disappears.

\section{Discussion and real-world applicability}
\subsection{Interpretation of the findings}
The current results support a predictive transition between a flexible
hybrid and a baseline-like model as regularization increases.
They do not show that linear effects are correctly allocated to the
linear branch or that regularization guarantees an interpretable tariff.
Because the branches can both represent some simple effects, their
decomposition is not uniquely determined by predictive fit alone.

\subsection{Frozen coefficients and total fitted effects}
Freezing the CANN's GLM coefficients preserves a reference component,
but those coefficients need not represent the complete fitted effects:
the neural correction may also capture linear effects.
Joint training permits coefficient adaptation, yet a penalty does not
guarantee an exclusively nonlinear correction.
Interpretation should therefore consider the combined predictor and
both branches rather than reading GLM coefficients in isolation.

\subsection{Calibration along the regularization path}
At $\lambda=0.0001$, test O/E is 1.0118 with deviance 45.104;
at $\lambda=0.001$, O/E improves to 0.9974 while deviance worsens to 45.727.
Aggregate balance and individual predictive fit therefore need separate
assessment.
Deviance is not a pure discrimination measure, and similar learning and
test scores do not establish stability across samples or random seeds.
Segment-level calibration remains an additional requirement.

\subsection{Limitations and robustness}
The main limitations are unequal feature access, different batch sizes,
validation reuse in initial fitting and preprocessing, incomplete penalty
coverage and a single seed and supplied test split.
Feature-level diagnostics were not conducted, so the experiments do not
establish whether regularization produces more interpretable fitted effects.
The reported deviances exclude regularization penalties and are directly
comparable across $\lambda$ values.
Within each run, however, the epoch restored by the callback is selected
using validation loss, which includes the penalty for regularized models.
Repeated seeds, comparable feature sets and independent model selection
would strengthen the comparison.

\subsection{Implications for insurance pricing}
A regularization path could help compare predictive gains with the
complexity of corrections to a familiar tariff structure.
Practical use would additionally require subgroup calibration, stability
checks, documentation of feature effects and review of the resulting
pricing decisions.
Aggregate O/E and Poisson deviance alone do not establish deployment
readiness.

\subsection{Modelling choices and development effort}
Practical model development involves a trade-off between feature engineering and hyperparameter tuning.
In this project, the GLM required explicit choices about feature transformations and age groups,
but fitting by maximum likelihood was comparatively straightforward.
The FNN reduced the need to specify functional forms in advance, although input preparation remained necessary.

As neural-network tuning was not the focus of this project,
most hyperparameters followed the example provided by
\emph{AI Tools for Actuaries}.

Nevertheless, substantial effort went into choosing batch sizes, epoch
budgets and regularization strengths.
In practice, hyperparameter tuning could require further experimentation
with learning rates, batch sizes, initialisation, regularization and
training procedures.
The optimisation-control experiments illustrate sensitivity to some of
these choices, although development time was not measured systematically.

The jointly trained hybrid does not eliminate either source of effort.
Its linear branch still requires a suitable GLM design, while its neural
branch retains the architecture and optimisation choices of an FNN.
Regularization strength adds a further choice.
The hybrid therefore controls the neural contribution without necessarily
simplifying model development.
Initialising the joint model with fitted GLM parameters, or using fixed
GLM predictions in the CANN, may nevertheless reduce the training needed
to reach a useful predictive fit compared with a stand-alone FNN.
Both hybrids start from the fitted GLM prediction rather than learning
the entire rate function from scratch.
Convergence time was not compared systematically in this project.
Its potential predictive gains must be considered alongside the effort
required for implementation, tuning and validation.

\section{Conclusion}
The implemented hybrid provides a tunable penalty on neural dense-layer
weights while allowing the GLM branch to adapt.
Available results show improved test deviance over the selected GLM
baseline at weak penalties and baseline-like performance at stronger
penalties.
The central claim about departure from GLM structure remains open until
the planned structural diagnostics are completed.
